# Proposed age pathways

These are course-design routes, not finished lessons or verified learner levels. The [catalog](mental-models.json) supplies a separate objective, learner action, observable check and transfer task for **every one of the 23 models in every band**. The clusters below make those 69 proposals navigable. Use diagnostics before choosing a route; age never supplies missing mastery.

The taxonomy supplies useful components, but not an entire Munger curriculum. Its maximum topic age endpoint is 15, and formal teen-level probability, economic reasoning, causal inference and model evaluation need original reviewed extensions. Lower-band activities introduce ideas concretely rather than claim that a six-year-old has mastered conditional probability or comparative advantage.

## Shared foundations and order

1. Describe an observation, distinguish it from a guess, and give a reason.
2. Represent quantities, choices and constraints; compare alternatives fairly.
3. Make a prediction, test or inspect evidence, and revise appropriately.
4. Track changes across time and connected parts.
5. Reason about uncertainty using counts, assumptions and suitable comparisons.
6. Combine models while inspecting motives, social influences and limitations.

This is a proposed conceptual order. The **stored taxonomy prerequisite graph** and a learner’s diagnostic responses still govern a generated unit. Model prerequisites are separately identified in the JSON: comparative advantage and sunk-cost work follow opportunity cost; confirmation work follows disconfirmation; interaction analysis follows incentives, social proof and elementary probability. A course outline does not authorize reversing stored topic edges or claiming every required skill is already present.

## Ages 6–8: explain, compare, try again

Keep numbers small and visible, read stories aloud when needed, and accept spoken or drawn explanations. Formal quantitative extensions stay deferred unless readiness is demonstrated. Use pretend materials and harmless physical tasks.

| Cluster and model IDs | Concrete experience | Observable check and new context |
|---|---|---|
| Seeing and knowing: `models-and-reality`, `circle-of-competence`, `latticework` | Compare a toy map with a classroom route; sort puzzles into can-explain, could-check and need-help; consider a plant using water and sunlight | Name one useful feature and one missing feature; give a reason for asking for help; apply two explanations to a new everyday problem |
| Choosing together: `opportunity-cost`, `scale`, `comparative-advantage` | Spend pretend picnic tokens; compare one craft item with ten; share two play roles | Name a feasible choice forgone, a shared tool bottleneck and whether a division of work helped both partners; repeat with time or a different game. Cooperation is an introduction, not a proof of comparative advantage |
| Checking and preventing: `falsification`, `confirmation`, `inversion` | Test safe paper shapes, inspect a contrary mystery clue, and compare toy bridge bases | Explain a changed guess using an observation; show how a prevention addresses one failure but does not guarantee success; transfer to a backpack plan |
| Change over time: `compounding`, `feedback`, `evolution`, `second-order-effects` | Compare add-two and double towers; trace a fictional watering loop; match creature traits to habitats; order a two-step story | Predict a small next step and explain its rule; distinguish a return loop from a chain; explain why a trait helps only in a context. Full population-selection mechanisms are deferred |
| Chance and buffers: `probabilistic-thinking`, `margin-of-safety`, `availability` | Draw and replace visible bag counters; compare a toy requirement with spare capacity; check a vivid card against a complete count | Use possible/more likely without promising an outcome; count before choosing “most common”; state what a buffer does and does not establish; repeat with a new bag or plan |
| People and choices: `misjudgment`, `incentives`, `commitment`, `authority`, `liking-disliking`, `social-proof`, `lollapalooza` | Use disclosed pretend posters, mascot cards, fictional crowd tallies and game rewards | Ask for a checkable reason, explain one influence and revise a harmless plan; identify two combined cues without claiming a psychological law; transfer to a new fictional advert |

## Ages 9–12: make the comparison explicit

Use small tables, short explanations and a learner-controlled rule. Show alternatives together. Separate what the learner observed from explanations the lesson proposes.

| Cluster and model IDs | Learner action | Check and transfer |
|---|---|---|
| Scope and evidence: `models-and-reality`, `circle-of-competence`, `latticework` | Test a travel-time model after adding an omitted wait; compare predicted confidence with explanations; use capacity and incentive lenses on a queue | Identify the failed assumption and where a model helps; explain a revised confidence judgment; change the queue’s constraints and compare which lens remains useful |
| Scarcity and cooperation: `opportunity-cost`, `comparative-advantage`, `scale` | Compare runner-up fair plans; inspect two-person production tables; allocate shared workshop tools | Identify the best feasible alternative and lower opportunity costs; explain a bottleneck; change a resource or production rate and reconsider rather than memorize an assignment |
| Tests and failure paths: `falsification`, `confirmation`, `inversion` | Select challenging rule-test cards, compare supporting and opposing evidence, and write a short project pre-mortem | Find a real counterexample, choose stronger evidence and trace each prevention to a cause; transfer to a new pattern or group event |
| Dynamic systems: `compounding`, `feedback`, `evolution`, `second-order-effects` | Compare fixed increments with proportional growth; manipulate a bounded loop; track a toy inherited population; trace participants’ later responses to a new rule | Explain the update rule, a return arrow, population versus individual change, and an uncertain causal link; transfer one model and explicitly state where its analogy fails |
| Uncertainty: `probabilistic-thinking`, `margin-of-safety`, `availability` | Compare short and longer repeated chance runs; choose spare supplies under uncertain attendance; compare headlines with a full fictional count table | Explain variation without a gambler’s-fallacy correction story; justify a buffer’s cost; use the correct denominator in a new event-rate problem |
| Judgment in groups: `misjudgment`, `incentives`, `commitment`, `authority`, `liking-disliking`, `social-proof`, `lollapalooza` | Compare reward rules, continuing versus switching a project, domain-relevant sources, name-blind evaluations, and message-source trees | Explain an unintended reward, ignore unrecoverable effort while honoring real obligations, distinguish repetition from independent evidence, and name multiple cues without inventing causal effect sizes |

## Ages 13–17: quantify, qualify and transfer

Check fractions, percentages, ratio reasoning, elementary probability and evidence evaluation first. A learner may use a simpler representation without lowering the requirement to justify a conclusion. New advanced objectives remain explicitly outside supplied taxonomy evidence when appropriate.

| Cluster and model IDs | Learner action | Check and transfer |
|---|---|---|
| Competing models: `models-and-reality`, `circle-of-competence`, `latticework` | Audit a toy predictor’s assumptions, record and score probabilistic factual forecasts, and compare causal/incentive/probability explanations of a fictional policy | Distinguish interpolation, extrapolation and causation; discuss sample limits and expertise boundaries; revise a model-selection memo for another setting |
| Allocation: `opportunity-cost`, `comparative-advantage`, `scale` | Allocate fictional hours, calculate opportunity costs and feasible trade ranges, and compare per-item costs under fixed setup and coordination costs | Separate sunk costs and absolute advantage; explain assumptions, switching costs and distribution; transfer to a team where learning or fairness may outweigh maximum output |
| Reliable tests and plans: `falsification`, `confirmation`, `inversion` | Precommit to outcomes that weaken a hypothesis, compare alternatives, and construct a failure tree | Identify discriminating observations, possible measurement errors and common failure routes; transfer to a new claim that resists refutation or an event plan with changed constraints |
| Dynamic consequences: `compounding`, `feedback`, `evolution`, `second-order-effects` | Use repeated percentage-change recurrences, vary delivery delays in a bounded loop, run a reviewed selection model and compare causal policy diagrams | Check invariants and sensitivity; distinguish population change from intention; explain uncertainty and why organizational analogies do not prove biological or moral claims |
| Uncertainty and robustness: `probabilistic-thinking`, `margin-of-safety`, `availability` | Use a 1000-case conditional-count grid, compare correlated versus independent backups, and inspect reference-class selection after a vivid anecdote | Use the correct conditional denominator; identify common-cause failures; avoid false precision and unjustified extrapolation; solve a new classification task with different prevalence |
| Interacting influences: `misjudgment`, `incentives`, `commitment`, `authority`, `liking-disliking`, `social-proof`, `lollapalooza` | Compare conflicting incentives, revise project choices transparently, evaluate relevant expertise, trace source independence and analyze a disclosed 2×2 toy dataset | Separate hypotheses about mechanisms from observations; distinguish additive effects from an interaction; propose a respectful, noncoercive evidence check; transfer to an unfamiliar fictional case |

## Culminating transferable performance

Choose a low-stakes fictional planning problem with limited resources, uncertain outcomes, multiple people and a measurable goal. Ask the learner to select **two or three** relevant models, represent their assumptions, make a choice, explain a forgone alternative, identify evidence that would change the choice, and then revise when one condition changes.

Accept a justified choice or a justified decision to seek more information; there need not be one predetermined “Munger answer.” Score observable reasoning separately: evidence use, feasibility, assumptions, uncertainty and transfer. Report what happened in this task, not a permanent intelligence, personality or mastery label. Physical transfer, classroom trials and long-term effects remain unrun until actually observed.
